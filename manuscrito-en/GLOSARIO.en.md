# Frozen decisions — US English edition

**Nothing in this file may be re-decided mid-translation.** Drift is almost always re-deciding on
page 90 something already decided on page 12. If an entry turns out to be wrong, it gets changed
*here first*, and then everywhere at once.

Market: **United States.** The book relocates — names, places and the employment framework of
chapter 14 all move. American spelling throughout.

---

## 1 · The framework terms — one word per concept, no synonyms

Synonyms "so it doesn't repeat" are the mechanism that broke the Spanish original once. Banned.

| what fills and drains (ch. 5) | depósito | **tank** | never *reservoir*, *account*, *store* |
| what empties a tank | fuga | **leak** | pairs with *tank* |

### 1.1 · Why *gate* and not *door*

Chapter 2 has five ladders (routes) and chapter 4 has three gates (conditions). In Spanish these
collided: the body of chapter 2 said "hay cinco puertas" while its title said "cinco escaleras",
and chapter 4 already used *puerta* for something else. It had to be fixed and is now guarded by
the `METÁFORA` check.

English makes that collision *easier*, because **"opens doors" is a far more common idiom in
English than "abre puertas" is in Spanish**. It would erode the framework line by line without
anyone noticing.

*Gate* is immune by construction: "opens gates" is not an English idiom, so the framework word can
never be diluted by accident. It is also a stronger image for what the chapter means — a
checkpoint that must be passed, not an opportunity that appears.

Tested against the load-bearing sentences:

- "Necesita que **tres puertas se abran a la vez**" → "It needs **three gates to open at once**."
- "las tres puertas fallan por motivos distintos" → "all three gates fail for different reasons"
- "Te falta una puerta, que es la segunda del capítulo cuatro" → "You're missing a gate — the
  second one from chapter four"

**The idiom "opens doors" is banned in the entire translation**, including where the Spanish uses
it loosely (ch. 14 "abre puertas —dentro y fuera—" → "gets you into rooms you couldn't get into
before").

### 1.2 · The refrain

Identical in chapters 1 and 4, in bold, and it cannot vary between them:

> **If they sat down tomorrow to talk about you, what would they have on that table besides your
> numbers and your boss's opinion?**

*On the table* is the right English idiom for exactly what the book means, so this one gets
stronger in translation, not weaker.

## 3 · Employment framework — chapter 14 and the three mentions of *convenio*

The Spanish text rests on the Estatuto de los Trabajadores. The US has no statutory right of
return and most states are at-will. **This makes the book's argument sharper, not weaker**: "lo
que no existe es el botón" is simply more true in the US.

| Spanish | **English** | note |
|---|---|---|
| interinidad / suplencia con fin fijado | **an interim or acting role with an end date** | common US practice, ports cleanly |
| periodo de prueba explícito | **a defined trial period with a review date** | ports |
| categoría (nueva) | **a new level** | *level* or *grade*; never *category* |
| el convenio (ch. 2, where the rules are written) | **the leveling guide** / **the job architecture** | US companies publish these; a *convenio* does not exist |
| la plaza (ch. 12, Marisol) | **the position** / **the opening** | |
| supervisora de área | **the nurse manager** | US hospital term |

The legal note in `16-fuentes` must be **rewritten, not translated**: at-will employment, no
statutory return right, and the same disclaimer that this is not legal advice.

out loud is the part almost nobody does.*

## 6 · Register

The Spanish has 545 second-person markers and no formal address. English has no formality fork,
but it has a worse trap: **corporate register**. That is exactly what this book defines itself
against on page one.

**Always:** contractions (*you're, don't, it's, isn't*). Plain verbs. Concrete nouns.

**Banned outright:** leverage · utilize · optimize · individuals (say *people*) · impactful ·
going forward · at the end of the day · circle back · reach out · touch base · deep dive ·
low-hanging fruit · best practice · synergy · stakeholder · empower · journey · unlock ·
game-changer · showing up as.

**The tone that must survive**, and it is not decoration — it is what separates this book from the
one it refuses to be:

- It concedes before it argues: *"and she wasn't entirely wrong"*, *"it's true and I stand by it"*.
- It never blames the reader. The enemy is missing information, not weak character. The epilogue's
  line is *"they're not doing anything wrong."*
- It says its reservations out loud: *"this is not a validated scientific model"*, *"that's
  1980s US and Canadian data"*, *"it's an observational study"*. **These are not padding and do
  not get trimmed.**

**The specific American risk is inflation of the tone** — bigger promises, harder imperatives,
subtitle-speak. A proposed Spanish subtitle was already rejected for this ("the hidden system
to… hack your company and get what you really deserve"). The same rule applies here.

Numeric target, from `registro.py` on the original: **15–18 words per sentence in all fourteen
chapters.** That is the most stable measure in the book. A chapter that comes out at 24 has
changed genre even if every sentence is fine.

## 7 · What must not move

Guarded by `check_manuscrito.py` — the word tables get translated, the logic does not change.

Seven things on the table (chs. 6–12, one per chapter, with the tally at each close). Five ladders.
Three gates. Three criteria. Five tanks. Eight signs. Five routes, five moves, three defenses,
three answers, three kinds of no. Forty-seven cross-references by chapter number. Five people in
the room. Marisol's four years, Jason's fourteen months, Hector's eleven years, Brian's six months.
The nine and five percent from Hamermesh and Biddle, the twenty from Wong and Penner — **and the
sentence explaining that they are not comparable.**
