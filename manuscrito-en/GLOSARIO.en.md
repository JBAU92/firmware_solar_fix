# Frozen decisions — US English edition

**Nothing here may be re-decided mid-translation.** Drift is almost always re-deciding on page 90
something already decided on page 12. If an entry turns out wrong, it changes *here first*, then
everywhere at once.

Market: **United States.** The book relocates — names, places and the employment framework of
chapter 14 all move. American spelling throughout.

> **Revision 2.** Three independent reviewers went over revision 1 before a single line of prose
> was translated. They found two errors in this file, about twenty collisions the first pass
> missed, and one live defect in the Spanish original. Everything below survives that.

### 1.1 · Why *gate*, and the one instance that must be rewritten

Chapter 2 has five ladders (routes); chapter 4 has three gates (conditions). These collided once in
Spanish and are now guarded by `METÁFORA`. English makes the collision *easier*, because **"opens
doors" is far more common in English than "abre puertas" in Spanish.**

*Gate* is immune by construction — "opens gates" is not an English idiom — and is a stronger image
for a checkpoint that must be passed. All fifteen framework instances were checked one by one;
three read *better* than with *door*: "bolted shut" (`04:127`), "pushing on the only gate that was
already half open" (`04:131`), "that one doesn't open by working harder" (`13:99`).

**Five loose *puerta*s that must not become gates or doors:**

| site | Spanish | English |
|---|---|---|
| **`09:41`** | "no solo **abre puertas**" — in the sponsor chapter, i.e. gate 3 | **"doesn't just get you considered for things"** |
| **`15:25`** | "empujando **la única puerta que ven**" — the epilogue payoff | deliberate choice: it echoes both frameworks. Use **"pushing on the one thing they can see"** — no door, no gate, no ladder |
| `07:182` | "se cierran estas puertas" | "the quiet way you stop getting asked" |
| `12:171` | "buscarle otra puerta" | "you have to find another way in" |
| `13:102` | "con un portazo" — three lines after `13:99` says *te falta una puerta* | "instead of storming out" |

`04:4-5` "llamar a puerta fría" → **cold calling**. No English door. Clean.
`10:12` "el portal interno" → **the internal job board**. Never *gateway*.

### 1.2 · Ladder — the exposure is the verb, not the noun

"Climbing the corporate ladder" is a tired idiom, but the book *attacks* it: `02:25-29` sets up the
single-ladder picture and knocks it down in the next line. The cliché is the setup. That only works
if the phrase never appears approvingly elsewhere.

- **`03:35` "La escalera de experimentos" is a second ladder with four rungs, one chapter after
  "five ladders". This is the original defect re-run.** It gets no ladder vocabulary at all:
  **"Testing it in stages"**, with *low-risk / medium-risk / high-risk* stages.
- **`subir` (28 instances) defaults to *move up / get promoted*, not *climb*.** Reserve *climb* for
  the ch. 2 setup, `02:49` ("the same climb, in another building") and `14:199` ("what you have to
  learn isn't how to climb. It's how to move"). Ladders 3, 4 and 5 can't be climbed anyway.
- **`02:42` "escalafón"** is the seniority ranking, not the ladder → **the ranks / your grade**.
- **`02:175`, `02:191` "camino"** is set *against* escalera, and *path/route* are banned →
  **"It was how he got there" / "The destination changes, not the work."**
- **`12:113` "el peldaño anterior"** = the step short of a promotion, not one level up →
  **"the step before it"**, not *rung*.
- **`trepa` → *a careerist*, never *a climber*.** `14:150` says "no es un trepa"; in a book built on
  ladders, "he's not a climber" hands the book's own frame to the villain.
- **The 22 instances where the Spanish already says *ruta*** (`01:229`, all of ch. 2, `05:262`,
  `08:155`, `12:84`, `12:149`, `13:101`, `14:138`, `14:207`) **all become *ladder*.** Rendering them
  as "routes" gives the book five ladders and five routes and makes the reader work out they are
  the same thing.

### 1.3 · The table — two banned collocations

*On the table*, *take it off the table*, *put it on the table* all work, and the refrain gets
stronger in English. Two idioms must be banned outright:

- **"brings something to the table" — banned.** It means *what a person inherently offers*, said of
  someone present. The book's thesis is the exact opposite: the table is a place you are **not**,
  and things get on it only because they were *put* there in advance. "What Dana brings to the
  table" is the "doing good work is enough" fallacy the book exists to correct. The Spanish never
  uses it; nothing is lost.
- **"a seat at the table" — banned.** It fuses *la sala* and *la mesa*, which the book keeps apart
  deliberately: the room is where you aren't, the table is where your evidence sits.

**Three *mesa*s that are not the table:**

| site | Spanish | English |
|---|---|---|
| `00:81` | "apostando en **la mesa más cara**" — a gambling table, 14 lines before the real one | **"betting on the most expensive game"** |
| `13:55` | "acaba **en tu mesa**" | **your desk** — "lands on your desk" would collide head-on with the refrain |
| `08:119` | "el estado de tus cosas —**tu mesa**, tu furgoneta" | **your desk** |

### 1.4 · The grid

Name it once, in chapter 1: *"HR people call it the nine-box."* After that, **the grid** — and never
bare "the grid" without an antecedent in the same paragraph, because in the US that is the power
grid. Cells are **boxes**, which ties back to *nine-box*.

### 1.5 · The chapter-2 worksheet — a third word was needed

Chapter 2's comparison device is called both *cuadrícula* and *tabla* in Spanish (`02:124`, `02:145`,
`02:148`, `02:154`, `02:156`). In English **"grid" is taken by chapter 1** and **"table" is the
refrain**. It needs a third word: **the worksheet**, which also matches the text's own instruction
("cópiala en un folio"). Its cells are **cells**, not boxes.

### 1.6 · The email — the framework object vs. fifteen ordinary emails

Spanish has two words, *carta* (framework) and *correo* (ordinary, 15 instances). English collapses
both. The framework object is always **the seven-line email**, with the five items attached.

- **`13:110` "un correo de tres líneas"** sits twenty-seven lines after `13:83` cross-references *la
  carta del capítulo siete*. Rewrite as **"three lines in writing"**, no *email*.
- **`11:130` "una propuesta de carta"** is **a proposed menu** — a restaurant menu, in a kitchen
  paragraph about tickets and food costs. A translator working mechanically from this table would
  get it catastrophically wrong.
- The five items are named **without the word *sponsor***, because chapter 9 hasn't defined it yet
  and the chapter-9 tally line says the fourth thing is "the first one that's a person instead of a
  piece of paper". See ANCLAJES § 8.

### 1.7 · File vs folder

Near-interchangeable in American English, and the book needs them apart:

- **file** = the personnel file. Chapter 1's title, and **`12:219` "una carpeta con el nombre de
  Javier" → "a file with Jason's name on it"** — a deliberate callback to chapter 1, which is the
  whole point of that sentence.
- **folder** = chapter 3's side project, and nowhere else.
- **`02:76` "la carpeta compartida de recursos humanos" → "the shared HR drive"**, so the reader
  doesn't meet "folder" a chapter before it's defined.

### 1.8 · Praise vs flattery

Chapter 8 runs on four words, not two. The breaking sentence is **`08:200`**: *"la versión buena, la
que funciona más que **el halago** y que nadie llama **adulación**"*. With one English word that
reads "works better than flattery and that nobody calls flattery." So: *halago* → **praise**,
*adulación* → **flattery**. That line becomes: *"the good version — the one that works better than
praise, and that nobody calls flattery, because it doesn't look like it."*

### 1.9 · Aspiration, not ambition

The book's own sources (`16:27-29`) cite the model in English: **ability, aspiration, engagement**.
Translating *aspiración* as *ambition* severs the link to the cited source, and collides with
*ambición*, which is a separate live word that titles a section in chapter 3. **aspiration**, always.

### 3.1 · Gate 2 cannot be called "an opening"

`04:100` defines gate 2 as *"**Oportunidad organizativa.** Que haya **sitio**. **Vacante**,
presupuesto, momento del año"*. Rendered naturally that is "an organizational **opening**. That
there's **room**. An **opening**, budget…" — the word twice, plus *room*, which is the book's
central image for something else entirely.

Gate 2 is **"an organizational slot"**. *Opening* is reserved for actual job vacancies. Its body:
**"That there's a slot. A vacancy, budget, the right time of year."**

The legal note in `16-fuentes` is **rewritten, not translated**: at-will employment, no statutory
return right, same disclaimer that this is not legal advice.

## 6 · Register

**Always:** contractions. Plain verbs. Concrete nouns.

**Banned — consultancy family:** leverage · utilize · optimize · individuals · impactful · going
forward · at the end of the day · circle back · reach out · touch base · deep dive · low-hanging
fruit · best practice · synergy · stakeholder · empower · journey · unlock · game-changer · align ·
drive (transitive) · learnings · actionable · add value · bandwidth · elevate · double down · lean
into · unpack · navigate (an organization) · robust · holistic · strategic (of a person) · north
star · skillset · upskill · take ownership · move the needle.

**Banned — LinkedIn / self-help family.** This is the one that would turn the book into what it
refuses to be on page one, and revision 1 missed it entirely:

- **visibility** — the book is *literally about being seen*; "increase your visibility" would appear
  in fourteen chapters. Always concrete instead: *so people see it*, *so your name gets said out loud*.
- **personal brand / branding** — the book is one sentence away from being a personal-branding manual.
- **level up** (verb) — collides with *ladder/rung* and with "the next level". The noun is fine.
- **mindset** · **growth mindset**
- **advocate for yourself / self-advocacy** — the US phrase for chapter 12, in therapy register.
  That chapter is called **Ask** for a reason.
- authentic / authenticity · hustle · grind · crush it · show up as.

**Banned — collocations that break a framework:** opens doors · brings ___ to the table · a seat at
the table · corporate ladder · climbing the ladder.

**Never use *table* as a verb.** In American English *to table something* means to postpone it.

**The tone that must survive.** It concedes before it argues (*"and she wasn't entirely wrong"*).
It never blames the reader — the enemy is missing information, not weak character; the epilogue's
line is *"they're not doing anything wrong."* It says its reservations out loud (*"this is not a
validated scientific model"*, *"that's 1980s data"*, *"it's an observational study"*), and **those
are not padding and do not get trimmed.**

**Sentence length: 13–16 words per chapter.** *Not* the Spanish 15–18. Spanish runs 15–25 % longer
than English, so chasing the Spanish figure means padding — the exact opposite of what the measure
is for. 24 remains the alarm.

## 7 · What must not move

Seven things (chs. 6–12, one per chapter, with the tally). Five ladders. Three gates. Three
criteria. Five tanks. Eight signs. Five moves, three defenses, three answers, three kinds of no.
Forty-seven cross-references. Five people in the room. Marisol's four years, Jason's fourteen
months, Hector's eleven years, Brian's six months. The nine and five percent from Hamermesh and
Biddle, the twenty from Wong and Penner — **and the sentence saying they are not comparable.**

## 8 · What the English `METÁFORA` check must contain

A literal port of the Spanish rules would be far too narrow; the English failure modes are
different ones.

- no `\bgates?\b` in ch. 2; no `\bladders?\b` in ch. 4
- `five gates` · `three ladders` · `three doors` · `five doors` — anywhere
- `open(s|ed|ing)? doors` — anywhere (catches `09:41`)
- `bring(s|ing)? \w+ to the table` · `seat at the table` — anywhere
- `corporate ladder` · `climb(ing)? the ladder` — anywhere
- `five (routes|paths|tracks|staircases)` — the *ruta* leak, 22 sites
- `\bthe ladder of\b` outside ch. 2 — catches the experiment ladder
- `\bon your table\b` — should be *desk*
- plus **`PARIDAD`**, which is the one that catches erosion of *five*, *three* and *seven* line by line
